Almost everyone who has an idea now has three ways to start. You can chat with Claude and get a small working page back. You can use an app builder that generates and publishes an app from your prompts. Or you can work with an AI team that builds the product and keeps it going. They sound similar. They are not the same thing.

The useful question is not "which is best?" but "what am I trying to do this week?" This guide is organised by goal, so you can pick the lightest route that gets you there and move to a heavier one only when you need to.

<p class="answer"><strong>The short answer.</strong> Use a Claude artifact to explore an idea, an app builder to get a first version online yourself, and an AI team when you want the product run and improved for you while you approve what goes live. They stack: many people use all three in order. Pick by goal and by who you want running it after launch.</p>

## The three routes in plain words

**A Claude artifact** is something Claude makes inside a conversation: a small page, tool or mini app you can look at, use and ask Claude to change. It is quick, free of setup and ideal for answering "would this even work?" It is a prototype that lives in the chat world, not a business with customers.

**An app builder** is a tool where you describe an app and it generates the code, a database and a place to publish it. You drive: every change is another prompt from you. You also make the choices around it, such as plans, domains and what to publish.

**An AI team** is a set of AI roles that share the work: a PM who asks questions and keeps the plan, engineers, a designer, QA and a data analyst. equipe is one example. You describe the goal and approve decisions; the team does the building, launching and following-up. We make equipe, so keep that stake in mind as you read.

## Decision guide by goal

| Your goal right now | Lightest route that fits | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Does this idea make sense?" | Claude artifact | Fastest way to see it; nothing to set up or pay for beyond what you already use |
| "Show it to three friends" | Claude artifact | A shareable prototype is often enough to get honest reactions |
| "Get a first version online that strangers can use" | App builder or AI team | You need hosting, a place for data and probably a domain |
| "Take payments or collect people's details" | Careful choice; see the security section | Money and personal data raise the stakes |
| "Run this as an actual small business over months" | AI team (or you plus a developer) | Someone has to watch, measure, fix and decide what is next |
| "I enjoy tinkering and want full control" | App builder | You steer every change |

If your answer is in the top two rows, stop reading and open a chat. You do not need a product yet; you need a better question.

## Route 1: exploring with a Claude artifact

**Good at**
- Turning a vague idea into something you can click in minutes.
- Cheap iteration: "make the form shorter", "add a second step".
- Learning what you actually want before you spend money.

**Limits**
- It is not, by itself, a place customers visit every day. A real product needs hosting (a computer that is always on), a database (where customers' information is stored) and a domain (your web address). Our [glossary of these terms](/blog/no-code-glossary-hosting-database-domain-secrets/) explains each one.
- Nobody is responsible for it once the chat ends. If something breaks next month, there is no one on call.
- Anything that remembers data between visits, sends email or takes money needs real infrastructure behind it.

**Who runs it after launch?** There is no launch. That is the point: it is the right tool for exploring and the wrong tool for being a business.

If an artifact has already proven the idea and you want to go further, read [Turn a Claude artifact into a live app](/blog/turn-a-claude-artifact-into-a-live-app/).

## Route 2: a first version with an app builder

**Good at**
- Going from prompt to a published app quickly, with hosting and a backend included.
- Letting you see every change as you ask for it.
- Keeping cost low at the start, since many builders have free or entry plans.

**Limits**
- You are the project manager, tester and operator. If you do not know what to check, you may miss problems until a customer finds them.
- Decisions pile up: plans, usage limits, domains, publishing settings, how to handle sign-in.
- "Who watches it next month?" is usually you.

Builders differ from one another on price, code export, security statements and what is included. We compared Lovable, Bolt and Base44, using only their official pages as read on 21 November 2026, in [equipe vs Lovable vs Bolt vs Base44](/blog/equipe-vs-lovable-bolt-base44/). The honest summary there is that builders are strong at fast first versions; the gap is what happens after.

**Who runs it after launch?** You do, or you hire a developer.

## Route 3: running a business with an AI team

**Good at**
- Asking you the questions you did not know to ask (who is it for, what should we check) before building.
- Splitting work among roles, with QA checking the work before it reaches you.
- Looking at how people use the product and proposing the next improvement.
- Handling the setup you would rather not touch: hosting, database and domain are set up by the team, so you never sign in to a code host, hosting service or cloud console.

**Limits**
- You give up some hands-on control. You are the decision-maker, not the editor.
- It is a newer kind of product. Some things are not shipped yet (see below).
- Pricing has two parts, a plan and credits for AI work. Every request shows its expected cost first and you set a monthly cap. The [cost breakdown](/blog/cost-to-build-and-run-a-small-app-with-ai-agents/) walks through it.

**Who runs it after launch?** The team, with you approving. Anything that goes live, costs money or reaches your customers waits for your approval as a short card with options, a recommendation and the expected cost. You answer with one tap. More on that in [what an AI startup team does](/blog/what-an-ai-startup-team-does/).

## The question people skip: who runs it on day 30?

Building is the exciting part and, honestly, the smaller part. After launch someone must:

1. Notice when something is broken.
2. Answer customer messages.
3. Look at whether anyone uses it.
4. Decide what to change next.
5. Pay for and renew the boring bits (domain, hosting).

Write the name of the person or thing responsible next to each line. If every line says "me" and you do not have the time, the route you picked may be too light. If you want to be hands-on, a heavier route may feel like overkill. The [build, measure, learn loop](/blog/build-measure-learn-with-an-ai-team/) shows what a healthy day-30 looks like.

## Safety when real people are involved

The moment a stranger types in an email address, or you take money, a prototype becomes your responsibility. Whichever route you choose, check:

- [ ] Where are people's details stored, and who can see them?
- [ ] Are passwords and API keys kept out of anywhere they could leak?
- [ ] Does anything spend money or message customers without you saying yes?
- [ ] Can you delete a customer's data if they ask?

How equipe approaches these questions is in [is equipe safe?](/blog/is-equipe-safe-data-money-approvals/) and on the [security page](/security/). Whatever you use, ask the same questions of it.

## A sensible path for most people

1. **Explore in an artifact** until you can describe, in two sentences, who it is for and what they do with it.
2. **Decide the route** using the goal table above and the day-30 list.
3. **Launch small.** One page or one feature, not everything.
4. **Measure before adding.** Our [30-day plan to paying customers](/blog/idea-to-paying-customers-30-day-plan/) shows one way to sequence this.
